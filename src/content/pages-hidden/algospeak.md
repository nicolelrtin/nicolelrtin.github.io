Relevant and articulate analysis of online linguistics and its relationship to profiteering algorithms.

I've had a self-professed enthusiasm for etymology since grade school, and have very much enjoyed @etymologynerd 's rise through the algorithm. It is certainly a novel experience to digest full-length text from someone I usually see in short-form "edutainment" content. The audiobook underscored that experience; I can't help but feel Adam adopted the same short-form-content dialect he uses to maximize attention. As he notes himself, it works.

Explanations are very clear and accessible, as I have come to expect from Aleksic. I suspect this will be a landmark analysis in the history of linguistics, as online dialects continue to evolve at an increasingly rapid pace. 

Around Chapters 4-5 though, I started wondering if I accidentally turned pages backward. This might speak to my familiarity with the subject, but the material began to feel redundant. In these chapters, he explicates how different groups have molded particular patterns of speech. Aleksic points out his fascination with the contribution of these various groups (African-American, LGBTQ+, kpop fandoms, swifties, gamers, techies, and anime fans) to the online square. Much of the chapter consists of examples that will be familiar to “zillenials”. Because of this, I'm not quite sure who the intended audience is. Those in my age group (mid 20s) literate online will find some interesting bits of information, while rest feels to me like a verbose history of common knowledge. Even the uninitiated may find initial explanations sufficient such that explicit callbacks feel like a regurgitation of previous points. The subsequent chapter, focusing on the mutual molding of incel culture and pop culture, makes this exploration more interesting, focusing on how language can uniquely gatekeep culture. The redundancy does not keep for long; Chapter 8 starts to move away from linguistics to discuss how meta-tags for groups and subcultures (formed from dynamics in Chapter 7) benefits corporate marketing, and also pigeonhole content variety.

I enjoy the personal touches and anecdotal flourishes in his writing. I admire the consistency of his voice in short-term to long-form content. Despite my criticisms, I'm surprised to find Aleksic is not affiliated with any academic groups currently, and I affirm this book is an astute articulation of internet dynamics of the past decade (2016–now). How often do we get a formal meta-analysis of the internet zeitgeist written in so faithful a voice by one of its own?
