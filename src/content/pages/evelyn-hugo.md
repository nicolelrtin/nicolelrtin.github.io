---
title: "The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo by Taylor Jenkins Reid"
description: "Book Review. Summer Reading 4/5."
pubDate: 2025-07-03
tags: ["books"]
---

With a few summertime days off and some curiosity for the Hollywood elite, I found this book to be an enjoyable read. As with most books, I didn’t fully digest the spine so– “historical fiction” yes; “romance” yes. There was a little less glitz and glamor than I expected. Way more cunning than I could imagine, and not in the way I assumed. 

Putting this book down, I wasn’t sure Evelyn Hugo was written with any character depth at all. Hugo is cunning and ambitious. She wants attention and she knows how to use her body as a pawn. She demonstrates a deep desire to control her narrative, recapturing media headlines to tell a version she wants to tell, the moment the narrative doesn’t go her way. In this way, it’s clear the stories narrated by herself are unreliable at best. The chapters are bit scandal-by-scandal. While an engaging read, I started to wonder what else the author had to say. The most I can say is, too late, Evelyn Hugo realizes she has cunning that exceeds the mileage of her sex.

Sexuality was well explored, and I liked the LGBTQ representation. I appreciated Reid’s WLW depiction. I do not know much about queer-platonic relationships, I would have loved to see this dynamic explored more. Reid depicts the subsequent complications well, especially regarding co-parenting and shifting between two properties. I loved how the 60’s setting created the central tension of “coming out” to the public, against pragmatic secret-keeping. While the narrator is undeniably ruthless, it is difficult to say that many of us in her shoes would trade our ambitions for a public relationship; Hugo’s values lie entirely around public opinion, and she knows the 60’s would not look kindly on homosexuals. Arguably, this is one of the most prominent aspects of the book, and one of its best parts. My ambivalence comes from the fact that this was not how the book was advertised. It’s a “plot twist” that comes in the first few chapters— I would have loved to pick this up for this reason, not despite it.

I liked the other characters in this book. I don’t know exactly why they surrounded her. The other characters were fun and interesting; it probably reflects Evelyn’s self centeredness that they didn’t have greater depth or lives apart from her, but combined with Evelyn’s one-track-mind, it can feel a little like lazy writing. I don’t know why the author decided she should kill literally everyone important off. It’s not clear that the mechanic of them all dying had any impact on Evelyn Hugo except to say she doesn’t deserve, and didn’t get, a happy ending. Perhaps it serves to reiterate how Evelyn took those around her for granted; but it could have been explored far more.

The strongest themes in this book surrounded ambition and intimacy, for the aforementioned reasons. 

There was an attempt to explore dual identities, in Hugo’s bisexuality and to a lesser (non)extent, Monique biracial background. This fell pretty flat to me, it felt like Monique’s presence was largely served to justify this point (I would say Monique serves to positively frame Evelyn’s cunning; to be a plot device; and to be biracial). Hugo’s sexuality is central to the narrative core, but Monique’s identity feels symbolic at best. Her race is acknowledged basically for exposition, and her personal experience is too fill-in-the-blank to carry weight. She could have been any race and the anecdotes would remain the same. This part hints at the author’s lack of familiarity in this domain, and thus I became acutely aware of the constructed message, which I don’t prefer. My take here would be to remove it entirely. Spoiler: I’m not sure if the author was depending on the audience to assume Harry Cameron’s lover was white as a surprise to us that it was instead, Monique’s black father. Similarly, I think there was something supposed to be said about Evelyn’s ethnicity (Cuban), but it only came up in the beginning and at the end, which was so jarring (where she’s making tortillas in Spain) that again, could have been left out.

To quickly summarize: the book was fun and had great LGBTQ representation. Evelyn Hugo is a cunning actress that falls in love with a woman unwilling to fully participate in her antics. Reid left a lot of messaging very clear to the reader, and in doing so flattened some emotional complexity. It could have been a stronger book if Evelyn had wrestled with her inner turmoil rather than passively experiencing tension. 

I would have loved to see Hugo realize halfway through the book that her cunning can go beyond the manipulating tabloids, and then watch her explore/fumble through what that means. Monique is used as a shallow projection of Hugo, which is a missed opportunity to give the former more weight. More, Hugo is, til the very end, concerned with public perception. The novel might have had lasting weight had we access to this evolution, to reconcile two things Hugo knows to be true: her closed love for Celia, and the judgement of the world around her. And since questions of forgiveness is raised in the middle and final chapters, I think this theme also could have benefitted an unhurried, more reflective treatment at the end.
