There is an abundance of high-quality college and career advice online and given by school resources. A lot of advice is repeated in consensus, which makes basic convention easy to find. Topics for college admissions, resume formatting, and interview preparation are well documented among troves of online resources.  
The lesser-repeated advice that I found useful, as an undergrad and for <a href="https://cdss.berkeley.edu/dsus/mentorshiphub">Berkeley DSUS mentees</a>, I have documented here. 

## 1. Networking is Making Acquaintances
"Networking" is a loaded term with good intentions. It makes a world of difference to know people who can vouch for your skills and interests. It is not easy to find these people as an undergraduate, especially when you are knee deep in classes and campus life. 

By nature, most of the people you meet you will never see again. Don't treat them like it! The important thing is to get out of your comfort zone and meet people in the chance that you may make a friend that understands you, and can *potentially* help you out. Going to office hours, and being friendly, will not guarantee you a connection with graduate students or a professor, but it opens opportunity for a shared interest, or a referral to someone else. Going to a recruiting event will not guarantee a connection, but you can learn about a new field or a new perspective to your career. 

A useful question to ask is always: 
<blockquote>"I'm curious to learn more about ___.   

Do you know anyone who could tell me more about __?"</blockquote>

The more you put your best foot forward, the more you will expose yourself to the world around you. 

Networking in college is hard. In my experience, I did not gain many professional connections through undergrad (if any). But, I do think it helped me to talk to new people (e.g. in my new job). Plus, I do think I learned more about career pathways through talking to people. 

### 1.1. what you can do for your "network"

If and when you do make a serendipitous connection, congratulations! 

Now what? It would be great if you had something to offer this person, but you're inexperienced and unspecialized. What you do have to offer, what *your* greatest asset is, is **your time**. As an empathetic person, you may start to hear their needs: typing lecture notes, formatting references, planning an event, reading a paper they don't have time for, etc. These examples lean toward academic or administrative help, which is an advantage for attending university. This is a real form of modern apprenticeship. By relieving small-scale burden, you allow yourself to "be in the room where it happens". While it may not be much now, your time is the greatest thing you have to offer to your "network".

## 2. "Modular Resume"
A brilliant suggestion I learned from a college friend is to maintain a "Modular Resume". This is not a one-page resume you send to others, this resume benefits just you. The modular resume should be an accumulation of every experience you've acquired, documented from several angles.

As you move through classes, volunteering, internships, you'll find one "resume" experience can be framed in different ways:
* Experience: CS61B
  * Technical: Programmed a deterministic agent to solve game trees by alpha-beta pruning in Java.
  * Teamwork: Led programming and oral presentation of a four-person team project (example)

Once in a while, take stock of the projects you have done and the causes you have spent time towards. Better yet, do it right after you complete something interesting or learn something new. Bullet point what you did, why you did it, how you did it, the effect you had, and skills you learned. Even if it is not resume-ready yet, you have a better basis for writing about it later. 

Now when you are applying for a job, cherry pick the experiences and bullet points that are most relevant to the role you're applying for. Customizing your resume for each role becomes a task of curation rather than de novo reframing. 

## 3. A Living Document for Long-Form Responses
**Save your responses**.  
I employed a similar technique for any long-form applications. I recommend maintaining a living document of your written responses and the prompts. As you apply for different opportunities, your throughlines will become more apparent. In other words, know thyself: be cognizant of your personal skills and values that you return to. 

When you land an interview, someone already likes what you wrote. Use your written responses as the foundation of your oral notes. As you prepare, your oral notes should live in this document as well. Like the "modular resume", you should have prepared answers that serve different roles, and correspond to your cherry-picked experiences. Similarly, cover letters and interview questions can be recorded here.

Be prepared for common interview questions[^1]. 
* Tell me about yourself.
* What are your strengths and weaknesses?
* Tell me about a time you had to work with a team and how you contributed.
* Tell me about X project?
* Why are you interested in this role?
[^1]: More thorough guides for responding to these questions are common internet topics.

Your living document should evolve with you. Your written and oral notes can work together to paint the mosaic of your different experiences and values. 


## Bonus: Applying to Jobs
What made a difference for me was applying to jobs while enjoying good music, TV, or an audiobook. Using the above suggestions, the process of customizing your application and resume for each role becomes trivial rather than de novo effort. Applying to jobs in this way was a "productive break" between studies as an undergraduate. If the application was more than a simple form, save the heavy lifting for later. 

There are many jobs that are not your dream job. If you could benefit at all from such an experience, I encourage you to apply. More interview practice and applications benefit both your application/interview skills, but also help you gain exposure to everything about post-grad life.
