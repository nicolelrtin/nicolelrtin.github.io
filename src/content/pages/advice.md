## 4. Networking = Making Acquaintances
"Networking" is a loaded term with good intentions. It makes a world of difference to know people who can vouch for your skills and interests. It is not easy to find these people as an undergraduate, especially when you are knee deep in classes and campus life. 

By nature, most of the people you meeet you will never see again. Don't treat them like it! The important thing is to get out of your comfort zone and meet people so you may *potentially* make a friend that understands you and *may potentially* help you out. Going to office hours, and being friendly, will not guarantee you a connection with graduate students or a professor, but you *might* discover a shared interest, or maybe they *might know* someone who can tell you more. Going to a recruiting event will not guarantee a connection, but you may learn something new about a new field or a new approach to a career. 

A useful question to ask is always: "Do you know anyone who could tell me more about __?"

The more you put your best foot forward, the more you will expose yourself to the world around you. 

Networking in college is hard. In my experience, I did not gain many professional connections through undergrad (if any). But, I do think it helped me to talk to new people (e.g. in my new job). Plus, I do think I learned more about career pathways through talking to people. 

If you do make a serendipitous connection, congratulations! When you are young, **your greatest asset is your time**. 

## 5. The "Modular Resume"
A brilliant suggestion I learned from a college friend is to maintain a "Modular Resume". This is not a one-page resume you send to others, this resume benefits you. The modular resume should be a living accumulation of every experience you've acquired, documented from several angles.

As you move through classes, volunteering, internships, you'll find one "resume" experience can be framed in different ways:
* Experience: CS61B
  * Technical: Programmed a deterministic agent to solve game trees by alpha-beta pruning in Java.
  * Teamwork: Led programmingimplementation and oral presentation of a four-person team project (example)

Once in a while (towards the end of the semester), take stock of the projects you have done and the causes you have spent time towards. Better yet, do it right after you complete something interesting or learn something new. Bullet point what you did, why you did it, how you did it, the effect you had, and skills you learned. Even if it is not resume-ready yet, you have a better basis for writing about it later. 

Now when you are applying for a job, cherry pick the experiences and bullet points that are most relevant to the role you're applying for. Customizing your resume for each role becomes a task of curation rather than de novo reframing. 

## 6. Passively applying to jobs
The world we live in now rewards the numbers game. When you have the requisite experience but lack the connections, diversifying your applications is the surest way to getting *a* job.

What made the difference for me was using the modular resume to mindlessly apply to jobs while enjoying good music, TV, or an audiobook. As an undergraduate, I adopted this attitude and treated it as a break between studies. While I do not entirely agree with this as a philosophy, it undoubtedly allowed me to get more interviews, practice interview skills, and expose me to enough careers to identify patterns and my preferences.
