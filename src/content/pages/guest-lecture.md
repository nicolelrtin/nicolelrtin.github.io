# Synopsis
The Guest Lecture is an interesting fiction book, tying in nonfiction ideas and intellectual exploration into the protagonist's insomniac stream of consciousness. I can't say this style will work for everyone, including myself, but it was a playful and engaging way to create a wide webbing network of concepts and mechanisms for which to present them.

# Thoughts
If you're coming to this book to learn about feminism, economics, or Keynes, you will not learn as much as the synopsis leads on. If you're well versed in these ideas, you may find the book's treatment of them more rewarding. Not having intimate enough grounding in specifics, I'm unsure of what true or novel ideas I learned in sum.

#### Women In STEM
In leagues of smart Women In STEM novels, this one felt the most relatable and realistic—I'm looking at you, Lessons In Chemistry. This may be my own failing to read more books in this category, but it feels like a failing of popular fiction as a whole. Riker uses the novel to play with Keynesian opinion and feminism in a way that arises from the protagonist's lived experiences. Her intellect is given in the way she thinks, the topics she wrestles with, and the nuance in her ideas.

Garmus' Lessons In Chemistry, on the other hand, conflates intellect with quirk, and presents an excessive use of elemental names ("pass the sodium chloride, please!") as a sign of an uncontained genius. Further, its handling of Women In STEM is far less nuanced. In Riker's novel, obstacles to academic advancement are painted as continuously moving goalposts, to which our protagonist must grapple with and persistently rises to meet. In Garmus'... it feels reduced to "who knew pretty girls can be smart" and "men are creepy". The effect reads as fetishized and preachy, regurgitating morals that may have been new in the 50s, especially compared to Riker's textured and modern account.

Comparisons aside, what Riker does well is paint a real portrayal of our guest lecturer's inner self. I enjoyed the competence of our protagonist, showed not just in her ideas but in the complexity of her self doubt. Doubts of what we achieve and the value of our merits, I believe, are one of the most significant personal battles we face in intellectual pursuits. Riker's exploration of authentic vs derivative work; academic tenure; the evolution of ideas; all felt thoughtful and well-developed. In this regard, the stream-of-consciousness structure worked well to weave in thoughts and memories in a natural, compelling way.
