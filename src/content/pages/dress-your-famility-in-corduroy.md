## Synopsis
A series of short stories chronicling his home life; moving states, a large upper middle class immediate family, a narcissistic stay at home mom, estranged distant relatives, emerging homosexuality.

## Spoilers
Some of his stories have sitcom-like writing, with the introduction of a drama/comedy (a secluded family, a hippie, a beach house) that later pointedly illustrates absurdities of everyday life. These stories end with a bit of a punchline, laying plain his message in an ironic resolution.

David Sedaris' voice is honest and naive, reflecting a child's logic in a way that is difficult to capture after years of learned maturity[^1]. He's able to write about morally questionable episodes. In the middle of the book, he’s able to write without judgement: it can be difficult to "see" these thoughts so plainly, but it invites reflection on questions of child sexuality and panhandling. This is one of those books I’d return to when I want to reconnect with the playfulness and openness of a child's mind, and also to reflect on how I relate to others. Towards the end, he leads you through his thoughts as his own values, self-consciousness, and lack of clarity muddle the morality of events that are benign (e.g. helping a boy out as a grown man). 
This section of the book, this evolution, hits home, illustrating how naivety is lost. The issue with growing up isn’t that we “see too much”, but that we develop values – and inevitably must wrestle with where we stand internally, and how the world will perceive us.

[^1]: This is what I liked most about Jeannette McCurdy's memoir, titled "I'm Glad My Mother Died". Bonus review: McCurdy puts us in the logic of her past-selves and writes with the clarity of the best authors. Rarely have I read the logic of our past-selves so clearly. Its clear that everything about this book stems from the vividness of her past, and the incredible amount of work she has put into healing and recognizing these behaviours. This should be textbook for eating disorders and narcissistic personality disorder.
